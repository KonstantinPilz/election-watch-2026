How this page works
  • Which races are listed. A race appears if its leading side traded below 90% at any hour in the past 7 days on Kalshi or Polymarket (hourly prices). Safe races still count toward the seat math; they're just not listed.
  • Prices. Each candidate's price is the midpoint of the best bid and ask, or the last trade when the spread is wider than 10 cents (Polymarket's own display rule). We rescale each race's prices to sum to 100%. Polymarket prices refresh in your browser every 30 seconds. Kalshi blocks browser requests, so a server job fetches Kalshi roughly every 5 minutes.
  • Senate seat math. 34 Democratic-caucus seats (including King and Sanders) and 31 Republican seats aren't up in 2026. For each of the 35 races up (33 regular, plus specials in Ohio and Florida), we take the probability that the non-Republican side wins. Independents who win (Osborn in Nebraska, Bodnar in Montana, Achilles in Idaho, Bengs in South Dakota) count on the Democratic side; none has committed to caucus with Democrats, so the Democratic "control" figure here is slightly optimistic. With Vice President Vance breaking ties, Republicans keep control at 50 seats; Democrats need 51.
  • Correlation. Treating races as independent makes the seat distribution far too narrow, because a national swing moves every race together. We use a one-factor Gaussian model: each race's outcome is driven by a shared national shock (weight √ρ) plus a race-specific shock (weight √(1−ρ)), calibrated so each race keeps its market probability. Auto mode picks ρ to best match the cumulative seat distribution in the Kalshi and Polymarket seat-count markets. The distribution is computed exactly (numerical integration over the national shock, then an exact count over races), not by simulation.
  • House seat math. Same method over all districts with a market (Polymarket covers essentially every district; Kalshi about 75). The seat-count markets are shown as horizontal bars spanning each bucket, at the per-seat average probability. Polymarket's market counts Republican seats; we convert to Democratic seats as 435 minus Republican seats. On November 1 the district markets summed to about 246 expected Democratic seats while both seat-count markets centered near 240–242, so the model sits a few seats more Democratic than the seat-count markets. Long-shot pricing doesn't explain the gap: safe Republican districts add about 4.6 "phantom" Democratic seats and safe Democratic districts about 4.1 phantom Republican seats, which roughly cancel. It's a disagreement between the two kinds of market.
  • Data. Kalshi public API and Polymarket Gamma/CLOB APIs. Market prices include fees and the long-shot bias, so treat small probabilities with caution.
